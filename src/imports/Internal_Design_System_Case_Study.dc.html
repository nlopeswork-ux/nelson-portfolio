Internal Design System

Building an internal design platform that unified structure and components across projects, cutting redundant rebuild work and accelerating delivery for both ambitious and lean engagements.

The Hook

As the team delivered a growing number of client engagements, a pattern became visible: many projects — despite serving different industries and clients — relied on very similar structures and components. Yet each one was rebuilt from scratch.

The root cause wasn't differing needs. It was that no project started from a uniform, autolayout-based foundation.

Every engagement inherited whatever ad hoc structure the previous file happened to have, so teams re-created grids, cards, tables and navigation patterns project after project — losing time that could have gone into solving the client's actual problem.

Rather than producing another one-off UI kit, the goal was to build a reusable internal foundation that could flex across two very different modes of work: short, lean projects and longer, more ambitious ones — without forcing either into the wrong mould.

Business Context

Business Goals

— Eliminate repeated structural rebuilds between projects

— Reduce time spent on initial project setup (the "blank canvas" phase)

— Create consistency of structure without limiting creative flexibility per project

— Enable the design team to scale — from small, short-turnaround projects to large, ambitious ones — using the same foundation

Constraints

Small team, no dedicated time: the system was built in parallel with live client delivery, never as a separate, fully-resourced initiative.

Two very different project profiles: short/lean engagements and large/ambitious ones both needed to be served by the same system, ruling out a rigid, one-size-fits-all kit.

Legacy inconsistency: existing files lacked uniform structure and autolayout, making migration to the new foundation non-trivial.

The Challenge

The challenge was never designing nicer components.

It was building a structural foundation flexible enough to support a two-week project and a several-month project with the same underlying system — without the team having to choose between speed and structural quality.

Discovery — Audit Methodology

Rather than starting from new designs, the process began with a structured audit of the existing project ecosystem, combining three methods.

1. Design / Interface Audit

A systematic inventory of screens, components and layouts across 15 projects delivered over the previous 3 years, gathered into a single visual reference to make repetition immediately visible rather than anecdotal.

2. Informal Observation

Reviewing how designers actually worked file-to-file, to see where time was really being lost — not just what was being rebuilt, but why.

3. Interviews

Conversations with designers and developers who had worked on those projects, to surface the practical frustrations behind the repetition — tight deadlines, inconsistent handoff, absence of autolayout.

Audit Dimensions
Components

Buttons, inputs, cards, tables, navigation, modals

Layouts

Dashboards, detail views, forms, listing pages, empty states

Structure

Grid usage, spacing, autolayout adoption, naming conventions

Key Insights

01

The majority of the 15 projects audited shared close to the same structural layouts (grids, headers, listings), regardless of client industry.

02

The main driver of duplicated work was not differing project requirements, but the absence of a consistent technical foundation — specifically, inconsistent or missing autolayout in source files.

03

Components were frequently rebuilt not because they needed to be different, but because they weren't easily findable or reusable across projects — there was no central library.

04

A modular, not rigid, system was needed to properly serve both short/lean and large/ambitious projects, without forcing every engagement into the same mould.

Design System Strategy

The system was intentionally split into two layers, so that consistency and flexibility could coexist rather than compete. The core structure never changed — only what sat on top of it did.

Core Platform (fixed)

— Foundations: colour, typography, spacing, grid

— Autolayout-first components (buttons, inputs, cards, tables, navigation, modals)

— Reusable layout patterns (dashboard, detail view, form, listing, empty state)

— Documentation and lightweight governance

Project Layer (flexible)

— Brand identity and visual theme

— Business-specific workflows

— Domain terminology

— Project-specific layout variations for ambitious, larger-scope work

Design Principles

Structure before style — autolayout and grid consistency come first.

Reusable, not rigid — flexible enough for both short and ambitious projects.

Documentation as part of the deliverable, not an afterthought.

Design–Engineering collaboration from day one, reflected in the cross-functional team.

Adoption & Flagship Example

A national cybersecurity reporting and monitoring platform is the clearest proof point for the system's impact.

4 months
initial scope
3 months
delivered, on the new foundation

Initially scoped at 4 months, the project was delivered in 3 months once built on the new foundation — a reduction of roughly 25% in delivery time, driven by reusable structure and components rather than by cutting scope. Beyond the timeline, the team reported clearer handoff between design and front-end, and faster alignment on requirements — since design and engineering were already working from the same structural language before the project began.

Rollout: the system is currently in pilot phase, adopted across internal projects, with the team validating the core/project-layer split before wider rollout. Each new pilot project feeds learnings back into the core platform, keeping it a living system rather than a one-time deliverable.

Impact

Business

Delivery speed: ~25% faster on the flagship pilot (4 months → 3 months), attributable to reuse of validated structure and components.

Reduced duplicated effort: no more rebuilding the same structures project after project.

Lower project start-up cost: a shared foundation reduces the effort needed to kick off a new engagement, whether short or ambitious.

Teams

Handoff: clearer, faster hand-off between Design and Front-End thanks to native autolayout components.

Alignment: faster requirement definition, since teams share a common structural and visual language from the outset.

Onboarding: new team members (including junior designers) ramp up faster against a documented, consistent system.

Technology

Scalability: a structure proven to work across both short-turnaround and ambitious, larger-scope projects.

Technical depth: the addition of a Senior Back-End Architect and Back-End Engineer extended the system's foundations beyond design into technical architecture considerations.

Leadership

Beyond executing the design work itself, this role led the definition of the system's strategy, its adoption path and its governance. This meant facilitating alignment between Design and Engineering as the team grew from 5 to 7 people, setting documentation standards, and establishing the core/project-layer split that let the system stay consistent while remaining flexible enough for very different project profiles — treating it as an evolving product, not a static component library.

Reflection

Building this system showed that the biggest obstacle was never visual — it was structural. The audit of 15 projects revealed that engagements across very different clients and industries shared far more than they appeared to: the differences were mostly in branding and business rules, while the underlying structural patterns were remarkably consistent.

That discovery made it possible to build a foundation flexible enough to serve both a two-week project and a multi-month one, without compromising either. The flagship result — a 25% faster delivery with better handoff and clearer definition — is the clearest evidence so far that a shared structural foundation, not just shared components, is what actually moves delivery speed. The next phase is to extend the pilot to more internal projects and start capturing the same before/after metric consistently across the portfolio.
